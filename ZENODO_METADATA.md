Dark matter is 26.8 per cent of the universe's energy density and 84 per cent of its matter. The density is known to two per cent. The particle is not known at all, and no dark matter particle has ever been observed.

This book is an assessment of the field as it stands in late 2026. It reports what the evidence establishes -- the cosmic budget measured from the acoustic peaks of the microwave background, the five independent handles on the missing mass, the Bullet Cluster, the small-scale structure that standard dark matter struggles to produce -- and what it does not. It takes the candidate classes one at a time, giving each the same treatment: the idea, the prediction, the cost so far, the result that would confirm it, and the result that would exclude it. It audits the anomaly record of the last five years, in which four significant claims -- the 3.5 keV line, the EDGES signal, the 21 cm anomaly and the SARAS 3 result -- did not replicate, and it applies the same evidentiary standard to the one live candidate, the single 248 keV event reported by LZ in September 2026 at 3.4 sigma local and 2.6 sigma global significance in an unblinded analysis.

The question in the title is answered in five senses, with the arithmetic attached to each. Dark matter has been used as a gravitational probe since 1933, at a precision that is unmatched by anything the field has attempted with a detector. It has been gravitationally captured by the Sun and the planets, where the local density is enhanced by four to five orders of magnitude and where the dark matter wind is a directed flux carrying information no other measurement can obtain. It has, on paper, been harvested: the arithmetic of the captured population is set out with the figure that makes the case, and the conclusion is that the coupling does not exist in nature, which is a decidable statement rather than an open question. Quantum devices respond to the ambient field. The particle does not, and the distance between those two facts is the distance the field has to close.

The origins question is treated as the hardest and most often evaded part of the subject. The relic density is a two-per-cent constraint on every production mechanism and on the cosmological history itself; the mechanism that explained the density with no free parameter has been excluded by the direct-detection nulls; and the mechanisms that remain either assume the density or tie it to an independently measured primordial spectrum. The book's conclusion is that the origins question is downstream of discovery rather than upstream of it, and that the cheapest decisive test is a measurement of the primordial spectrum and the CMB spectral distortions.

Part VI converts the assessment into a programme: why the answer must be a portfolio of differentiated experiments rather than a race, a candidate-by-candidate decision criterion set with kill conditions attached, a statement of what a discovery would and would not prove, and a roadmap to 2040 with the experiments that should occupy it.
